# DEVLOG

One entry per learning, architectural change or improvement. Newest at the bottom.

---

**Scoped the research agent to a fixed allowlist rather than open search.** Six sources, host-checked at fetch time, each carrying a license and a citation. The alternative — searching for whatever looks useful — would make the data layer unreproducible and would quietly invite exactly the scope drift the spec's guardrail exists to prevent.

**Chose Owens Valley as the primary theatre, but only after measuring it.** The AOI was picked for relief (Sierra crest to the west, White/Inyo range to the east, valley floor between) and then *tested*: 3396 m of relief and a monotonic masking gradient. A theatre is only worth anything here if terrain genuinely occludes, and that is a measurement, not a judgement call.

**Replaced a degenerate masking statistic with a real viewshed.** The first version asked "what fraction of the AOI is below the highest peak", which is trivially 100% and told us nothing. Replaced it with the actual per-ray LOS test from a summit sensor over 1500 random points. That is what produced the number the whole project rests on: 76% of the AOI is masked at 100 m above ground, 0% at 3000 m. The lesson is that a statistic which cannot come out badly is not evidence.

**Line-of-sight returns a signed clearance, not a boolean.** A hard 0/1 mask gives the policy no gradient — an aircraft 5 m below the sightline and one 500 m below look identical. Returning the minimum ray-above-terrain margin in metres makes the sign the constraint and the magnitude the learning signal.

**Cache keys embed an AOI bounds fingerprint.** Found this the expensive way: widening the AOI returned the previously cached DEM in 0 s and the extent statistics silently did not change. Without the fingerprint, every downstream LOS ray, spawn point and threat placement would have been computed against terrain that was not there.

**Cross-checked the DEM against published airfield elevations.** Two independent sources — 3DEP terrain and OurAirports field elevations — agree to within 3.6 m at every field in the theatre. This is the cheapest possible validation of the WGS84 → UTM reprojection that every LOS ray depends on, and a datum error would have shown up as tens of metres.

**The local atmosphere is not the textbook atmosphere.** Intending only to get air density for the airframe model, the pressure-level profile also yields the refractivity gradient, and therefore the effective-Earth factor the LOS model needs. Measured: −32.0 N-units/km, giving k = 1.256 against the standard 4/3 = 1.333. Dry high-desert air refracts less, so the radar horizon is genuinely shorter here than the default assumption. The LOS model takes k as a parameter and defaults to the measured value.

**Threat classes declare a design range; the range equation solves the power.** The first attempt hand-set transmit powers and apertures and produced a 1296 km detection range against a 1 m² target — unphysical, and beyond the horizon anyway. Inverting the problem means the only invented number per class is a deliberately round, role-based engagement distance, and everything else (RCS^(1/4) range scaling, frequency-dependent gaseous attenuation, the shape of the Pd ramp) follows from physics.

**Swerling 1 rather than a non-fluctuating detection model.** An aircraft's radar cross-section swings by an order of magnitude with small aspect changes. A non-fluctuating model turns detection on almost as a step (Pd 0.9 → 0.1 across a 1.2× range ratio); Swerling 1 spreads it across 2.2×. The blurrier edge is the physically right one *and* the one that gives the policy a continuous exposure gradient instead of a cliff.

**Albersheim's approximation now refuses to extrapolate.** Its argument goes negative outside roughly 0.1 ≤ Pd ≤ 0.9, where it was silently returning a domain error mid-inversion. It now raises with a message naming the validity band, and the inverter builds its grid only over the valid range. Returning a fabricated number outside a model's validity band is worse than failing.

**Validated the coordinated-turn relation against real traffic rather than asserting a g-limit.** 1459 consecutive ADS-B pairs of the same aircraft give a finite-difference turn rate that the instantaneous state vector does not carry. Inverting ω = g·tan(φ)/V puts implied bank angles at p95 9.1° and max 26.8° — exactly the range airliners fly. The relation the airframe model is built on is therefore confirmed against measurement; the manoeuvre limit itself remains an explicit design choice, flagged as such, because civil traffic never banks hard.

**The env reads component specs, never the cache.** `naigos/data/theatre.py` assembles everything the simulation is parameterized by from `components/*.json`. That indirection is what makes every number in the simulation traceable to a source and a sha256, and it keeps the training path free of any network or geospatial dependency.

**Edge sampling returned NaN on the southern and eastern grid boundaries.** `TerrainGrid.elevation` tested the *interpolable interior* (`r0 < rows-1`) rather than the raster footprint, so a point sitting exactly on the far edge came back as no-data and the ENU resampler then had to paper over a hole it should never have seen. Now "inside" means inside the bounds, and the outer half cell clamps to the edge cell.

**Two agents built overlapping layers; recorded rather than silently merged.** `naigos/data/dem.py` and `naigos/data/cache.py` arrived from the env side while the research layer was being built, duplicating the cited cache and the DEM path. The research-side resampler was therefore parked at `naigos/data/enu.py` instead of overwriting anything. The seams are listed in the handoff notes below; they are design decisions for a human, not something to reconcile unilaterally.

**Confirmed a vertical-mirror bug at the env/data seam.** `py3dep.get_dem` returns row 0 = north (y descending, EPSG:5070) — verified empirically, not assumed. `naigos/env/terrain.py::sample_height` indexes `hmap[y0, x0]` with `fy = y/cell`, so rows must increase with +y, i.e. row 0 must be south. `naigos/data/dem.py::load_dem` takes `raw.values` and resamples in index space with no flip and no reprojection out of Albers. The heightmap the env flies over is therefore mirrored north-south, and stretched anisotropically. This is precisely the bug class that never announces itself: the terrain still looks like terrain, LOS still returns plausible numbers, and the policy still learns something.

**Resolved the duplicated data layer by deleting the env-side copy, not by merging.** `naigos/data/dem.py` and `naigos/data/cache.py` were written from the env side in parallel with the research layer and duplicated the cited cache, the DEM fetch and the resampler. They also carried the vertical-mirror bug identified above. Both are gone; `naigos/env/theatre_bridge.py` now goes through `naigos/data/enu.py::real_terrain`, which has the correct row-0-is-south convention and a real reprojection. Deleting the newer, wronger copy was cheaper and safer than reconciling two resamplers.

**Calibrated the detection model before tuning a single reward weight.** Two calibration bugs were dominant and neither would have been visible from the reward curve. First, `p_kill` was applied as a per-step probability while being documented as per-second, so the entire world's lethality scaled silently with `dt`; it now compounds as `1-(1-p)^dt`. Second, `lethal_range` and `detect_range` were within a factor of two, which means an aircraft is already inside the envelope by the time it is detected — there is no standoff band to route through and the task degenerates into a coin flip regardless of policy. The gap between "seen" and "shootable" *is* the problem being solved.

**Measured the headroom before trusting the environment.** Over 64 worlds at curriculum level 0 on the real theatre: a naive direct route survives 39% of sorties, a crude terrain-following controller 50%, and a hand-written avoid-plus-nap controller 78%. Mean detection probability falls from 0.27 to 0.14 when flying low. An env where a hand-written policy cannot beat the baseline is an env where RL has nothing to find, and that is worth knowing before spending a GPU hour.

**The policy learned to fly into a mountain to break radar lock.** Terrain loss was priced at 50 against a shootdown at 200, so crashing was literally the cheaper way to stop being seen; terrain deaths climbed to 88% of sorties while the shootdown rate "improved" to 0.11. Every way of losing the airframe — shot down, terrain, out of bounds, out of fuel — is now priced identically, and `test_reward_shaping.py` asserts it. A falling shootdown rate is not progress if the aircraft is still lost.

**Shaping that integrates larger than the task reward replaces the task.** With per-step exposure and lock weights of 1.0 and 2.0, a 500-step episode accumulated roughly 700 in shaping cost against 320 of achievable progress-plus-arrival reward, so the optimal policy was to die on step one. The weights are now set so that flying the entire route fully exposed still beats being destroyed, and that inequality is a test rather than a hope.

**A fixed-metre boundary ramp broke when the map changed size.** A 10 km soft ramp sat inside the spawn inset on the 190 km synthetic map but outside it on the 97 km real theatre, so on the real theatre every aircraft started inside the penalty region carrying a constant offset it could not escape. Both the ramp width and the spawn inset are now fractions of the map extent. Anything geometric has to scale with the map or it encodes one particular map.

**Civil ADS-B calibrates what airliners choose, not what an airframe can do.** OpenSky's climb-rate p95 is 13.3 m/s. Over the Owens Valley DEM's 3.7 km of relief a terrain-following controller with that limit flew into the Sierra on roughly 80% of sorties, because it physically cannot climb a ridge it is already committed to. Climb rate and flight-path-angle limits are now named `ASSUMED_*` constants that state they are tactical design choices; the measured civil value is recorded next to them as the thing they deliberately override.

**The CBF's vertical barrier needed its own class-K gains.** Sharing `alpha1`/`alpha2` with the horizontal envelope barrier capped descent at 8 m/s while 6 km above the ridgeline, because the two channels have timescales two orders of magnitude apart — a 200 m/s closing geometry against a 1 s flight-path-angle lag. With a separate `alpha1_terrain`, the gain reads directly as "allowed descent rate per metre of clearance", which is a quantity that can be sanity-checked by hand.

**The barrier is in distance form because the squared form is numerically indefensible at these ranges.** With `h = |d|^2 - r^2`, both `h` and `hdot` scale with the square of a 10^4-metre range, so a class-K gain tuned at 1 km is wrong by four orders of magnitude at 100 km, and the filter either never engages or saturates. In distance form every term is in metres and metres per second and `alpha1 * h` reads as the closing speed the barrier will tolerate.

**Threat kinds became data so the theatre could define them.** The env originally hard-coded a three-value enum with an `is_interceptor` check for altitude behaviour. The research layer's `model.detection` component describes five calibrated radar classes, so kinds are now a tuple of dataclasses carrying `airborne`, `spawn_weight` and `label`, and the observation width follows `len(cfg.threat_kinds)`. Adding a sixth class is now a data change.

**Lethal reach has to be annealed, not just detection reach.** The medium-SAM class has a 49.5 km lethal radius on a 97 km-wide theatre, so at full scale two sites blanket the map and curriculum level 0 is already unsurvivable — there is no gradient for the policy to climb. The red curriculum now scales lethal range from 0.45 to 1.0 alongside detection range and threat count.

**Two bugs surfaced by reading training runs, not by reading code.** The red curriculum promoted on `1 - shootdown_rate` as a stand-in for survival — but a policy that has traded being shot down for flying into a ridge has a *low* shootdown rate, so it was promoted into a harder theatre, survival collapsed from 0.51 to 0.10, and the run never recovered. Separately, the CMDP cost channel summed a once-per-episode terminal event with a per-step indicator, so "expected violations per agent-episode ≤ 0.05" was arithmetically unmeetable and the Lagrange multiplier could only integrate upward. Neither is visible in the source; both are obvious in a metrics table with the death causes broken out.

**A keep-out filter discharges the constraint into whatever it was not told about.** Wiring the CBF with envelope and terrain barriers but no map boundary made the trained policy *worse*: it satisfied envelope keep-out by leaving the map, bounds losses tripled from 28 to 88 of 192 sorties, and net survival fell from 0.651 to 0.474. Four half-space edge barriers fixed it. The general lesson is that a barrier set has to be complete over the ways an episode can end, not just over the hazard you were thinking about.

**The backstop helps a naive policy and costs a trained one.** Against the converged checkpoint the CBF removes terrain losses entirely (20 → 0) and cuts shootdowns (19 → 12), but objective rate falls from 0.651 to 0.240, because its conservative margin closes corridors the policy had learned to thread. Against a hand-written nap-of-the-earth controller the same filter nearly doubles survival (0.31 → 0.61). That is `prompt.md` §6's "a backstop, not the plan, and its value is bounded by how good the learned policy already is" showing up as a measurement instead of a disclaimer.

**Mean exposure conditioned on survival is a survivor-biased metric.** It flatters a policy that dies early, because its surviving steps are all near the start of the route where nothing can see it yet — which is why the naive direct route appeared to have *lower* detection probability than a policy that was clearly evading better. Evaluation now also reports a fixed 100-step window over every agent alive or dead, and exposure among sorties that actually reached the objective.

**The policy climbs over the threats instead of hiding under the terrain, and that is a real tactic rather than a hack.** Mean AGL rises from 2878 m to ~5400 m over training. Three of the five theatre-calibrated threat classes have engagement ceilings between 4.5 and 6 km, so climbing above them makes those classes unable to engage at all, at the price of being brightly visible to two long-range classes that cannot reach you either. The agent found "trade detection for un-engageability" from local observations alone. Terrain masking demonstrably works and is available — flying low halves detection probability on this DEM — so this is a statement about what the reward prices, not about what the physics permits. Recorded as a specification decision rather than quietly patched.

**The ray march, not the terrain grid, was the binding constraint on line-of-sight — and then it turned out both were.** `los_samples` was fixed at 24 regardless of grid size, so a 128 km ray was sampled every 5.3 km and walked straight past ridges. Measured over 2000 rays on the Tehran DEM: at 1500 m cells, visibility falls 0.1985 → 0.1830 going from 24 to 96 samples. The first version of this note concluded that grid resolution therefore "bought nothing" — which was measured on high-altitude rollouts, where it genuinely does not. At a fixed 96 samples, refining 1500 → 500 m moves visibility −6.0 pp, about three times the sample-count effect. The coarse grid had been smoothing ridges away and over-reporting visibility by roughly 50% relative at low altitude. Both changes were needed and the one I had dismissed mattered more.

**Correcting the model made the policy look better, not worse.** Re-measured on Owens Valley at 500 m / 96 samples, the trained policy now beats the hand-written avoid-plus-nap heuristic on survival (0.677 against 0.646) where it previously lost (0.651 against 0.688). Detection falls for every policy. Worth recording because the temptation with a re-measure is to check whether the headline still holds and stop there; the interesting result was in the comparison that flipped.

**Vertical exaggeration scaled the globe mesh but not the entities on it.** Cesium's `verticalExaggeration` applies to the terrain surface, `Model` and 3D Tiles — not to `Cartesian3.fromDegrees`. I had defaulted the viewer to ×2, which rendered every aircraft one to four kilometres inside the mountain, made occlusion unconditionally true, and inverted the precise claim the viewer exists to demonstrate. It looked plausible on a static screenshot. Now ×1 by default with every altitude routed through a single exaggeration-aware helper.

**A feature can be gated off in exactly the case it exists to demonstrate.** The line-of-sight ray was drawn only when track quality exceeded a threshold — but track quality decays toward zero the instant an aircraft is masked, so the green "a ridge is cutting this ray" state was very nearly unreachable. Selection now runs on detection probability, which carries the LOS term directly. The measured split is 5% masked, 56% grazing, 40% clear; before the fix the first of those was invisible.

**A recording is positions in a local frame, and that frame is not optional.** Replaying an Owens Valley rollout while the server was configured for Tehran placed the entire sortie in Iran, smoothly and with nothing looking wrong. Recordings now carry their theatre, georef and cell size, and a mismatch is refused with the command that fixes it. The same class of bug also had replay serving the live environment's terrain rather than the surface the rollout actually flew over.

**Serving the DEM the viewer draws is not the same as serving the surface the model used.** The first version of the terrain endpoint resampled the cached 30 m DEM, which diverges from the env's own 500 m grid by up to 2094 m — reintroducing the exact defect the endpoint was written to remove. Sampling the env heightmap instead, with the clamp-at-edge behaviour `sample_height` already has, brings it to mean 1.65 m. Filling outside the area of interest with zero rather than clamping had put a 2 km bilinear seam along the boundary.

**Imagery and terrain are two layers, and only one of them is evidence.** The viewer had ion imagery and the simulation's heightmap arriving through the same code path, with `createWorldImageryAsync` as the token-gated upgrade — which is Bing Aerial, Cesium's default. Two problems in one line. Licensing: Bing is third-party commercial data metered by session under Microsoft's terms, an odd thing to ship in a portfolio project when Copernicus Sentinel-2 (ion asset 3954) is ESA open data, free to *use* rather than merely free to view. Correctness: the globe's relief is the surface line-of-sight was computed against, so the moment imagery and elevation can come from the same provider, E-9 is one config change away from returning. They are now separate constructions with separate sources, the ion token is read from `NAIGOS_CESIUM_ION_TOKEN` rather than a flag default, and `tests/test_imagery_layers.py` asserts that no ion *terrain* provider exists and that nothing under `naigos/env` or `naigos/rl` names imagery at all. The structural test is the one that matters: a comment saying the policy never sees satellite pixels is worth less than a test that fails if it ever could.


**A Modal wrapper that has never been run is a plan, not a path — and the honest fix is instrumentation, not a benchmark.** `modal_train.py` had an image, a Volume and an entrypoint, and every number in the repo was still from CPU. The temptation was to launch it and quote a speedup. What it needed first was the ability to tell a real GPU run from three things that look identical to one: a run truncated by the six-hour timeout, two runs interleaved into a directory named by hand, and a worker where JAX silently fell back to CPU. That last one is the dangerous case — the loop completes, the timings look like timings, and a CPU number gets published as a GPU number. So `perf.json` now records the backend and device kind alongside first-iteration compile time, median and p90 seconds per iteration, env-steps/s and peak device memory, `run.json` is written once and refuses to be overwritten by a different configuration, and `scripts/modal_runs.py verify` fails a Modal-launched run whose backend was CPU. The CPU baseline it needs to be compared against is now measured too: 0.54 s/iteration and 3.8k env-steps/s at 32 worlds × 64 steps. Still no GPU run, so still no speedup claim.

**Timing a JAX loop with a stopwatch measures dispatch, not compute.** Dispatch is asynchronous, so closing the timer straight after `train_step` returns times the enqueue. The loop already pulled its metrics back to the host on the next line, which is the actual sync point, so the timer closes after that conversion and the reported seconds-per-iteration are real. Two consequences fell out of it. The first iteration is compile plus one step and is labelled as such rather than folded into an average. And the curriculum's unconditional jit rebuild every ten iterations (next-steps.md G-4) turned a known inefficiency into a measured one: those iterations are booked as compilation instead of quietly inflating throughput, and `perf.json` reports how many there were and what they cost.

**The Volume was committed once, at the end.** A Modal Volume only persists what has been committed, so a run that hit its own six-hour timeout — precisely the long run this path exists to make possible — would have left an empty Volume and nothing to fetch. `run()` now takes an `on_persist` callback fired after every history, perf and checkpoint write, and the Modal wrapper passes `volume.commit`. The same seam is what lets a local run write the identical files with no callback at all.

**The cheap run has to be a gate, not a suggestion.** The failures that only appear remotely — a dependency missing from the image, an unmounted data cache, an unwritable Volume, no GPU actually attached — are all catchable in three iterations at 16 worlds. So `--profile full` is refused unless a smoke run verified against *this commit* is recorded on the Volume; a code change re-arms the gate, because a code change is what reintroduces those failures. It can be overridden, and the override says so out loud.

**A checkpoint that holds only the policy is an export, not a save point.** The checkpoints written before this carried the actor, the critic, the multiplier's pre-activation, the curriculum level and the iteration number — everything the demo needs to fly a route, and not enough to continue training. Resuming from one would have silently reset both Adam states, the multiplier's *own* Adam state, the RNG stream and the reward curriculum, while producing a `history.json` that reads as one continuous run. The optimizer-state loss is the sharp one: Adam's bias correction thinks it is at step 1, so the first updates after a "resume" are effectively at a different learning rate, and the multiplier is an integrator, so losing its state loses the accumulated price of the cost constraint. Schema-2 checkpoints carry all of it — and the fix is only believable because it is falsifiable: `tests/test_resume.py` runs four tiny iterations straight through, runs two and resumes, and asserts the two learners agree to *zero* absolute difference across every parameter, both optimizer states and the multiplier. Bit-identical or it is not a resume. The top-level `actor`/`critic` keys stayed exactly where they were, because the demo reads `pickle.load(f)["actor"]` and a format change that breaks the demo to fix training is not an improvement.

**`modal run` is not detached, and the difference is the whole feature.** The entrypoint blocked on `.remote()`, so the six-hour run this path exists for died with the laptop lid. Backgrounding the client would not have fixed it: Modal tears down an *ephemeral* app when its client exits. What actually detaches a job is `.spawn()` on a **deployed** function — the call enters Modal's queue, Modal's infrastructure executes it, and the returned call id is a stable handle that outlives the client entirely. So the flow gained a `modal deploy` step and `scripts/modal_runs.py` gained `submit` / `status` / `logs` / `cancel` / `resume`. The old attached entrypoint stayed, because watching a traceback arrive is still the fastest way to debug an image; it is just labelled as what it is.

**A status that can only be read from one side is not a status.** The obvious design is for the worker to record its own state. It cannot: a container killed by its own timeout, or preempted, or cancelled, never gets to write anything, so its manifest says `running` forever and every dead job reads as live. The other obvious design — ask Modal — knows the container is gone but not whether a single checkpoint was committed first. So `classify_status` merges both, with a stated precedence: a worker-recorded terminal reason wins, because the worker was there; otherwise an upstream terminal state overrides a live manifest and says out loud that the worker was killed without being able to record why; and a live manifest with an hour-old heartbeat and no upstream signal is `unknown`, not `running`, because reporting `running` there is a claim the evidence does not support. `resumable` is deliberately a flag rather than a seventh state — a run can be timed out *and* resumable, and it can be failed and *not* resumable because it died before its first checkpoint. Forcing those into one enum would require lying in exactly the case that matters.

**Auto-retry on a long training job is a way to spend twice.** Modal offers retries and it was tempting to set them, since the failure being defended against is a container disappearing. But its retry restarts from scratch: the same iterations get paid for again, and the new container writes into a run directory that already has a writer, interleaving checkpoints under one `run.json`. `retries=0` stays until resume is complete enough to be automatic, which it now is — and it still stays, because deciding to spend another six hours should be a decision. What the failure mode did earn is a writer lock on the run directory and a refusal to submit a second job for a run whose manifest is live. `history.json` is rewritten whole at every eval boundary, so two writers do not merge; the later one erases the earlier one's curve.

**"Resume" is where a configuration change turns into a fabricated result.** Continuing a run under a different commit, seed, theatre or batch shape produces a `history.json` whose halves came from different experiments and which is indistinguishable from a real curve once plotted. So resume compares the relaunch against the run's own immutable `run.json` and refuses on any of twelve identity keys — and the override is per key, because a blanket `--force` would also wave through the theatre change nobody meant to make. The most recent checkpoint is not simply taken, either: selection walks backwards from the newest, because the newest file is exactly the one a killed container was most likely mid-write on. And everything a resumed run emits says so — `perf.json` carries the resume chain, every `history.json` row after the splice carries the iteration it resumed from — because a `perf.json` whose timings cover only the second half of a run must not read as the timings of the whole run.

**`history.json` ended at the last eval boundary, which made a completed run and an interrupted one indistinguishable.** With `eval_every=20` and 1000 iterations they coincide, which is why it was invisible; at any other pairing the file simply stops early and nothing says whether that was the end of the run or the end of the process. The final iteration now always evaluates, so the last row is the iteration the run claims to have reached and `verify_run_dir` can compare the two.

**Terrain resolution is nearly free and the ray march is what costs — the opposite of how the knob had been treated.** Cell size had been chosen the way a cost is chosen: 1500 m for training, 500 m for the demo, on the assumption that a finer DEM buys accuracy at the price of throughput. Measured across two AOIs, four cell sizes and three march lengths (`scripts/bench_terrain.py`, 64 worlds × 128 steps, best-of-9 timings, 2000 rays per row against the native 30 m DEM): at 96 samples, throughput is flat within noise from 1500 m to 100 m cells — relative 1.00 / 0.98 / 1.00 / 0.93 on Owens Valley, 1.00 / 1.03 / 0.99 / 1.03 on Tehran — while the heightmap grows 230×, from 14 KB to 5.4 MB. Doubling `los_samples` halves throughput (1.00 / 0.54 / 0.30). The grid is a bigger array to gather from; the march is more gathers, and only the second is in the hot loop. The two knobs had been priced as if they were the same knob.

**And the coarse grid, not the march, was the binding line-of-sight error.** At a fixed 96 samples, refining 1500 → 100 m cuts the false-visible rate against the source DEM from 3.65% to 1.05% (Owens) and 5.35% to 1.40% (Tehran), for no throughput. At a fixed 1500 m grid, raising 96 → 384 samples moves the same rate only 3.65% → 3.15% and 5.35% → 4.90%, for 3.4× the compute. A ridge that resampling has already averaged into the valley is not recovered by sampling the ray more finely. This sharpens the earlier entry that concluded "both mattered and the grid mattered more": at *equal cost* the grid is the only one that matters, and the false-visible error is one-directional — a smoothed grid over-reports visibility, which inflates measured exposure and understates the exact mechanic the project is about.

**Two resolutions, because it is two questions.** Recommended **physics: 100 m cells at `los_samples = 96`** — the only configuration passing a 2% false-visible budget on both AOIs, at 0.93× baseline throughput. Recommended **presentation: 100 m served at `/terrain n = 1024`** — at the shipped `n = 512` a 100 m env grid renders with a p95 of 25.5 m against a 30 m AGL floor, so the aircraft draws inside the hill it is hugging; `n = 1024` brings that to 9.6 m at a 2 MB payload. The constraint on the presentation side is the endpoint's lat/lon resample, not the DEM, which means E-12's warp-table fix would remove it entirely rather than being a nicety. **Neither is applied.** Every committed RL number was produced at 1500 m / 96 (500 m / 96 for the published re-measure and the live demo) and still is; adopting a recommendation means re-running them, and `tests/test_bench_terrain.py` fails if a default moves without that happening.

**The measurement design cost two rewrites, both caught by numbers that moved when nothing had.** Throughput was first gated against the fastest row in the sweep, which produced a `0.85x best < 0.85x` rejection — a ratio against a sweep maximum follows whichever row the scheduler favoured, so it is now taken against the baseline configuration the published results came from. And `throughput_stats` reported medians while another job shared the laptop: the same configuration came back at 186k, 153k and 139k agent-steps/s across three runs, enough spread to invert the recommendation. Interference only ever adds time, so the headline is best-of-N with the spread reported beside it. A benchmark whose estimator is noise-sensitive is a benchmark that recommends whatever the machine was doing.

**The march-step heuristic predicts the opposite of what was measured, and is reported as an advisory rather than a gate.** At 100 m cells and 96 samples the ray steps 13.5–17.4 terrain cells per sample on the theatre diagonal, which says most of that grid is never looked at — and that configuration is nonetheless the most accurate one measured, at full speed. Gating on it would have rejected the winner. The likely reason is that a bilinear 100 m surface preserves ridge *height* even when sampled sparsely, while a 1500 m surface has already lost the ridge; but that is a hypothesis, so the diagnostic is printed next to the outcome it contradicts instead of overruling it. `gaps.md` B-3 records the test that would settle it.
